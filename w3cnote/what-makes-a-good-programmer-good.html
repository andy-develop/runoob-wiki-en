What makes an excellent programmer excellent?

Category

Over the years I have worked with many programmers; some of them are extremely skilled, while others seem mediocre. Not long ago, because working with some highly skilled programmers felt so pleasant, I spent some time thinking about what I admire in them. What makes excellent programmers so excellent, and bad programmers so bad? In short, what makes a programmer excellent?

Based on my experience, becoming an excellent programmer has nothing to do with age, education, or how much money you earn. It all comes down to your approach, and more deeply, your mindset. I've noticed that the programmers I admire all share some similar habits. It's not their knowledge of the languages they choose, nor a deep understanding of data structures and algorithms, nor even years of work experience. Rather, it's their way of communicating, their way of managing themselves, and the way they talk about programming with their refined technical skill.

Of course, there are more qualities required to become an excellent programmer, and I cannot judge a programmer solely by the presence (or absence) of these traits. But I know it when I see it—when I see a programmer with these traits, I think, "This person really knows what they are doing."

Excellent programmers do research

Or call it "look before you leap," or call it "Google it."

No matter what you call it, most programming problems you are likely to encounter have already been solved in some form. Ecclesiastes recorded long ago that there is nothing new under the sun. Excellent programmers know to do research before solving problems, whether through GitHub libraries, web blogs, or by communicating with experienced programmers.

I have seen that even excellent programmers can quickly find solutions, but the bad programmers I have worked with never seek help from others, resulting in a lot of duplicated work or incorrectly solved problems. Unfortunately, they eventually pay the price for the mistakes they make.

Excellent programmers read error messages (and act on them)

This includes parsing stack trace information. Yes, this is a very ugly and unfortunate thing. But if you are not willing to do it, how can you know where things went wrong? The effective programmers I know are not afraid to dig into problems. Ineffective programmers see an error but are unwilling even to read the error messages. (This sounds ridiculous, but you would be surprised how often I encounter it.)

Furthermore, excellent programmers solve problems as soon as they spot them. Reading the error message is only the beginning for them; they are eager to dig deeper and find the root cause. They do not like to shift blame, but are willing to find solutions. Problems stop with them.

Excellent programmers read source code

Documentation, tests, teams—these all lie. Even if not intentionally, if you want to know exactly how something works, you have to look at the source code yourself.

Don't be afraid if it is not the language you are best at. If you are a Ruby programmer and you suspect a bug in a C library in Ruby, crack it open and look. Yes, you may not be able to get the source code, but who knows? You just might. You have a better chance than if you never try at all.

Unfortunately, if you are in a closed-source environment, this becomes very difficult, but the principle remains the same. Bad programmers have no interest in looking at source code, and as a result, problems bother them for much longer than those who are willing to look at the source code.

Excellent programmers just do it

Excellent programmers tend to take initiative. They have an uncontrollable urge inside them; when they identify a problem or discover a new requirement, they immediately implement a solution, sometimes too early, sometimes too aggressively. But their instinctive reaction to a problem is to tackle it head-on.

Sometimes this can be very annoying, but their enthusiasm is an important part of doing things well. Some people may procrastinate, avoid problems, or wait for problems to disappear on their own, while excellent programmers solve them right from the start. In short (or obviously), if you see someone eagerly investigating and solving problems, you probably have an excellent programmer on your hands.

Excellent programmers do not avoid difficulties

This is often a trait of bad programmers: they easily jump from one problem to another, moving on to the next before truly understanding the current one. They blame bugs in the program and then spend a lot of time debugging code that already works well. They let emotions take the lead and trust intuition rather than careful, rigorous analysis.

If you rush through solving a problem, or even treat every problem as a world-shocking disaster, you are likely to make mistakes or fail to solve the underlying problem. Excellent programmers take the time to understand what went wrong, even when disaster strikes; but more importantly, they treat ordinary problems as important ones to be solved, so they solve more problems more accurately, and by doing so, they do not raise the tension in the team.

Excellent programmers communicate well

After all, programming is a form of communication. Writing code is like writing prose; being able to express your ideas concisely is very important. I have found that programmers who can write concise emails, elegant status reports, or even just an effective memo will also be excellent programmers.

This applies to writing code and English alike. It is certainly possible to write a line of code with parentheses, brackets, and single-letter functions, but what is the point if no one understands it? Excellent programmers take the time to communicate their ideas through various channels.

Excellent programmers are passionate

I think this may be the most important aspect of an excellent programmer (and perhaps this also applies to fields other than computer science).

If you truly care about what you do, if you treat it not as a job but as a hobby, an interest, or something very engaging, then you have an advantage over others in that field. Excellent programmers keep programming all the time. Average programmers work eight hours a day and have no side projects, nor any interest in giving back to the community. They do not constantly try new methods, nor do they become obsessed with programming languages just to see how they work.

When I see a programmer spending weekends on projects they love, participating in creating tools they use every day, and devoting themselves to new and meaningful things, that is when I am convinced that the person before me is amazing. In the end, excellent programmers see their career not only as a way to make money, but also as a way to make a little difference in life. I think that is the real reason that makes the best programmers excellent. For them, writing code is a way to change the world, and that is why I deeply respect and admire them.
